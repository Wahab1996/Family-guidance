Definitions & Classification

Core definitions (adults)

  • ACC/AHA 2017: HTN = persistent SBP ≥ 130 mm Hg and/or DBP ≥ 80 mm Hg.
  • JNC 8 / ISH: HTN = persistent SBP ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mm Hg.
  • Primary (essential) HTN: no identifiable cause (~90 % of adult cases).
  • Secondary HTN: due to specific underlying condition (renal, endocrine, vascular, etc.).
  • Resistant HTN: BP ≥ 130/80 despite ≥ 3 drugs, or needs ≥ 4 drugs to control.

Classification (ACC/AHA 2017)

Category SBP (mm Hg) DBP (mm Hg)
Normal < 120 < 80
Elevated 120–129 < 80
Stage 1 HTN 130–139 80–89
Stage 2 HTN ≥ 140 ≥ 90

Pediatric definitions (brief)

  • < 13 years: use percentiles (HTN ≥ 95th percentile for age/height/sex).
  • ≥ 13 years: thresholds similar to adults (≥ 130/80 for HTN).

Epidemiology & Risk factors

Epidemiology

  • Common: affects about one‑third to half of adults in the US; prevalence increases with age.
  • Most adult cases are primary HTN; secondary HTN ≈ 10 % of adult cases.
  • Very common in overweight/obese patients; prevalence > 60 % in obese groups.

Risk factors for primary HTN

Non‑modifiable

  • Positive family history.
  • Race/ethnicity (higher in African American populations).
  • Advanced age.

Modifiable

  • Overweight / obesity (most important modifiable factor).
  • Diabetes, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome.
  • Smoking, excessive alcohol intake.
  • High sodium / low potassium diet.
  • Physical inactivity, psychosocial stress.

Etiology & Secondary Hypertension

Primary (essential) HTN

  • Multifactorial: genetic, epigenetic, environmental factors.
  • Pathophysiology: ↑ total peripheral resistance and/or ↑ cardiac output.

Secondary HTN – red flags

  • Severe HTN, resistant HTN, or hypertensive emergency.
  • Onset < 30 years or new diastolic HTN > 65 years.
  • Target organ damage disproportionate to BP level.
  • Drug‑induced HTN, unprovoked/sig. hypokalemia.
  • BP difference between arms (suspect aortic dissection / coarctation).

Mnemonic – RECENT causes

  • Renal: renal artery stenosis, CKD, GN, ADPKD, etc.
  • Endocrine: Cushing, hyperthyroidism, Conn (primary hyperaldosteronism), pheochromocytoma, hyperparathyroidism, acromegaly, CAH.
  • Coarctation of the aorta.
  • Estrogen: OCPs.
  • Neurological: ↑ ICP, stimulants.
  • Treatment/substances: glucocorticoids, NSAIDs, decongestants, cocaine, amphetamines, alcohol, nicotine, caffeine.

Key specific secondary causes

  • Primary hyperaldosteronism: resistant HTN ± hypokalemia; high aldosterone/renin ratio.
  • Pheochromocytoma: paroxysmal HTN, headaches, palpitations, diaphoresis.
  • Renal artery stenosis: abdominal bruit, rise in creatinine after ACEI/ARB; asymmetrical kidneys.
  • Obstructive sleep apnea: obesity, snoring, daytime sleepiness; nondipping BP pattern.
  • Coarctation: arm–leg BP difference; diminished femoral pulses.

Clinical Features

Typical presentation

  • Usually asymptomatic until end‑organ damage or acute rise.
  • Early nonspecific symptoms: morning headache, dizziness, tinnitus, blurred vision, palpitations, chest discomfort, epistaxis, fatigue, sleep disturbance.

End‑organ involvement (overview)

  • Brain: stroke, TIA, hemorrhage, cognitive decline.
  • Heart: LVH, CAD, MI, HF, AF, aortic aneurysm/dissection.
  • Kidneys: hypertensive nephrosclerosis → CKD, ESRD.
  • Eyes: hypertensive retinopathy (arteriolar narrowing, hemorrhages, exudates, papilledema).

Diagnosis & Initial Workup

Screening

  • Adults ≥ 40 years or any age with risk factors: annual screening.
  • Adults 18–39 years with normal BP and no risk factors: every 3–5 years.
  • Office BP: elevated average on ≥ 2 readings on ≥ 2 visits suggests HTN.

Confirming the diagnosis

  • ABPM (preferred): BP every 15–30 min over 12–24 h.
  • HBPM: repeated home measurements after proper training.
  • Use out‑of‑office BP to confirm before starting long‑term therapy when possible.

Initial evaluation for newly diagnosed HTN

History & physical

  • BMI, waist circumference.
  • Cardiac and neurological exam; check for bruits, edema, pulses.
  • Look for signs of secondary causes (endocrine stigmata, renal disease, etc.).

Baseline labs

  • Fasting glucose ± HbA1c.
  • Electrolytes (Na, K, Ca).
  • Renal function (creatinine, eGFR).
  • Lipid profile.
  • TSH.
  • Urinalysis + urine albumin/creatinine ratio.
  • ECG; consider echocardiogram and fundoscopy when indicated.

When to look for secondary HTN

  • Resistant HTN or abrupt/worsening control.
  • Early onset (< 30) or late diastolic onset (> 65).
  • Unexplained hypokalemia, renal bruit, asymmetric kidneys, endocrine signs.

Important Subtypes

White coat vs masked hypertension

Subtype Office BP Out‑of‑office BP Notes
Sustained HTN Elevated Elevated True hypertension.
White coat HTN Elevated Normal Often due to anxiety; monitor progression with ABPM/HBPM.
Masked HTN Normal Elevated Risk ≈ sustained HTN → treat as HTN.

Isolated systolic hypertension

  • Definition: SBP ≥ 140 with DBP < 90.
  • Etiology: decreased arterial compliance with aging; high output states (anemia, hyperthyroidism, AR, AV fistula).
  • Treatment: lifestyle + thiazide or dihydropyridine CCB; goal SBP < 140.

Management – General Approach

Stepwise approach (ACC/AHA 2017‑aligned)

  1. Lifestyle changes for all with SBP ≥ 120 or DBP ≥ 80.
  2. Assess 10‑year ASCVD risk and comorbidities.
  3. Decide on pharmacologic therapy based on BP level and risk.
  4. Choose appropriate first‑line drug(s).
  5. Titrate/adjust to reach target BP, with regular follow‑up.

Non‑pharmacologic measures (approximate SBP reduction)

  • Weight loss to ideal body weight (~ 1 mm Hg per kg in overweight).
  • DASH diet: fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low saturated/trans fats (≈ 11 mm Hg).
  • Reduce sodium (< 1500 mg/day if possible) (≈ 5–6 mm Hg).
  • Increase potassium (3.5–5 g/day) if no advanced CKD (≈ 4–5 mm Hg).
  • Limit alcohol (≤ 2 drinks/day men, ≤ 1 women) (≈ 4 mm Hg).
  • Exercise: aerobic 90–150 min/week; + dynamic/isometric resistance (≈ 4–8 mm Hg).
  • Smoking cessation for ASCVD risk reduction.

Indications to start medication

  • SBP ≥ 130 or DBP ≥ 80 with:
    • Clinical ASCVD (CAD, PAD, stroke, HF), or
    • 10‑year ASCVD risk ≥ 10 %.
  • All adults with SBP ≥ 140 or DBP ≥ 90, regardless of risk.

Choice of initial therapy

First‑line classes

  • Thiazide diuretics (chlorthalidone, HCTZ).
  • ACE inhibitors (lisinopril, enalapril, captopril).
  • ARBs (losartan, valsartan).
  • Dihydropyridine CCBs (amlodipine, nifedipine).

General rules

  • Stage 1 HTN (130–139/80–89) → usually start with monotherapy.
  • Stage 2 HTN (≥ 140/90) and ≥ 20/10 above goal → start with combination therapy (e.g., ACEI/ARB + CCB or thiazide).
  • Black patients (without HF or CKD) → thiazide or CCB preferred as initial agent.
  • Never combine ACEI + ARB or ACEI/ARB + direct renin inhibitor (risk hyperkalemia, renal dysfunction).

Second‑line / add‑on agents

  • Beta‑blockers (cardioselective/non‑selective; labetalol/carvedilol) – first‑line only when compelling indications (IHD, HF, AF, aortic disease, etc.).
  • Loop diuretics – preferred when GFR < 30 mL/min or HF with volume overload.
  • Aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone) – primary hyperaldosteronism or resistant HTN.
  • Other: alpha‑1 blockers, alpha‑2 agonists, hydralazine, minoxidil – adjuncts in selected cases.

Resistant hypertension – approach

  • Rule out pseudoresistance (bad technique, non‑adherence, white coat effect).
  • Optimize lifestyle and discontinue contributing drugs when possible.
  • Ensure thiazide (preferably chlorthalidone) is included; add aldosterone antagonist.
  • Evaluate for secondary HTN and consider referral to HTN specialist.

Targets & follow‑up

  • Most adults: BP < 130/80 (individualize in elderly/comorbid).
  • Follow‑up ~1 month after initiating/increasing meds, then every 3–6 months when stable.
  • Monitor electrolytes and creatinine especially after ACEI/ARB or diuretic initiation.

Special Patient Groups

Older adults

  • Very high prevalence (> 60 % ≥ 60 years); often isolated systolic HTN.
  • Record orthostatic vitals at diagnosis; beware of falls and hypotension.
  • BP thresholds for treatment variably 130–150/90; individualize using shared decision‑making.
  • Avoid alpha‑blockers and loop diuretics as chronic first‑line agents due to orthostatic risk when alternatives available.

Children and adolescents

  • Most HTN in older children/adolescents is primary; in young children, often secondary (renal, coarctation, etc.).
  • Screen high‑risk children every visit; others annually from age 3.
  • Pharmacologic indications: persistent HTN despite lifestyle, stage 2 HTN, symptomatic HTN, HTN with CKD/diabetes, or LVH.
  • Preferred drugs: ACEI/ARB, thiazide, or long‑acting CCB (amlodipine); beta‑blockers not first‑line.

Pregnancy

  • Manage according to hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (separate guideline).
  • Avoid ACEI, ARB, direct renin inhibitors; options include labetalol, nifedipine, hydralazine (for acute/severe).

Complications of Chronic Hypertension

Cardiovascular

  • Hypertensive heart disease: LVH → HF (HFrEF/HFpEF), dilated cardiomyopathy.
  • Coronary artery disease and MI.
  • Atrial fibrillation.
  • Aortic aneurysm and dissection.
  • Peripheral artery disease; carotid stenosis; accelerated atherosclerosis.

Neurologic

  • Ischemic stroke and TIA.
  • Intracerebral hemorrhage (e.g., basal ganglia hemorrhage).
  • Subcortical leukoencephalopathy; cognitive decline.

Renal

  • Hypertensive nephrosclerosis: afferent arteriolar narrowing → ischemia → FSGS → CKD/ESRD.
  • Early: microalbuminuria and microscopic hematuria; later: progressive proteinuria and rising creatinine.

Ophthalmologic

  • Hypertensive retinopathy: arteriolar narrowing, AV nicking, hemorrhages, cotton‑wool spots, exudates, papilledema in severe cases.
